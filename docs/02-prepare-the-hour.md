# Chapter 2 — Prepare the Hour

One good hour is built the day before. This chapter is the checklist: what to
sort out in advance so the session itself feels effortless and unhurried.

## 2.1 Pick the right person (for your *first* helping run)

If you're new to helping, start with someone who is:

- **Curious, not cornered.** Someone who's said "I should try that" at
  least once. Avoid the person you most want to convince; save them for after
  you've had a few practice runs with a willing learner.
- **Available.** One hour, uninterrupted. A friend with fifteen minutes between
  meetings is a session waiting to fail.
- **Safe to struggle with.** You'll both be awkward. Pick someone you've been
  awkward with before.

## 2.2 Book the hour like it matters

Vague plans die. Say the whole plan out loud:

> "How about Saturday morning, one hour, over coffee? We'll do one real thing
> on that AI program I use, and if you like it we can do more, and if you
> don't, no harm done."

Names a time. Names a length. Names the deliverable. Names the exit. No
surprises.

## 2.3 The physical setup

Run through this the night before:

- [ ] **The machine they'll use.** Their laptop or tablet if possible — never
      your machine as the main seat. Familiar keyboard = familiar territory.
- [ ] **Charged and plugged in.** A low-battery warning at minute four poisons
      the mood.
- [ ] **Signed in, before they arrive.** If an account is needed, create it
      *with them* or have them type the password into a box they can see. Never
      type a password for them (see §2.6).
- [ ] **Big enough text.** Bump the browser zoom to 110–125% for the session.
      You can lower it later; seeing clearly up front is caring, not babying.
- [ ] **Quiet seat, good lamp, drink nearby.** The couch beats the desk chair.
- [ ] **Your phone on silent, face down.** Modeled attention.
- [ ] **A notebook and pen.** For them, old-school, for writing down anything
      they want to remember. Paper is the most underrated AI accessory.

## 2.4 The content setup

- [ ] **Pick ONE task** (Chapter 3). Written on your side of the table, not
      shown yet. One backup task, in case the first one lands in five minutes.
- [ ] **Site open and loaded.** Don't make them watch a progress bar while
      wondering if they're wasting your time.
- [ ] **Examples ready.** If the task needs "something to write about," bring
      three tiny starters ("what's a good gift for a niece who loves dungeons
      & dragons?") in case they freeze at a blank box. Freezing at the blank
      box is the single most common moment of session one.
- [ ] **Know your exits.** Which two AI tools will you use? Don't tour them
      all. One tool, well used, is the whole first hour.

## 2.5 Protect the hour

- Tell household cohabitants: "We're doing a thing from 10 to 11, please don't
  interrupt unless the house is on fire."
- Put your own errands on the other side of the hour. Nothing says "this isn't
  important" like ducking out to move the laundry.
- End on time, even if things are going *great*, especially when things are
  going great (Chapter 6). Leave them hungry.

## 2.6 The password-and-privacy moment (do it right the first time)

Sooner or later there's an account. Handle it with these habits from day one:

1. **They type it. You look at the ceiling.** Passwords are personal property.
   Turn the "I'll do it" into "you've got this" and glance away with a smile
   while they type.
2. **No password into your hands, ever.** Not in a note to you, not "so you
   can fix things later." If they offer, say: "Better if you keep it — that
   way nobody can touch your stuff but you."
3. **Explain privacy in plain terms, briefly.** One or two sentences is
   plenty: "What you type here gets sent to a company's computers, so as a
   rule, don't put in addresses, medical numbers, or passwords-in-disguise.
   We'll practice telling what's okay to share — that's a skill, and you'll
   get it fast."
4. **Never connect anything for them.** Email, contacts, calendar — let them
   click each connect, with the question always available: "hey, does this
   seem like something you want it to know?"

Getting this right at the start is what makes the handoff at the end
(Chapter 7) clean. If they own the account, they own the journey.

## 2.7 Prepare yourself, not just the room

Ten quiet minutes the night before:

- Read Chapter 4's section about *this* learner type.
- Remind yourself of their two latest wins (after session one).
- Decide your opening sentence (Chapter 5, §5.2) and say it out loud once.
- Tell yourself the low-expectations line until *you* believe it. The calmer
  you are, the faster they learn — it's the least fair fact of teaching, and
  it's true.

## Chapter 2 in one breath

Book it like it matters, set up their machine with dignity, load one task and
three sparks, protect the hour, and handle the password moment like the
integrity moment it is. Prepared rooms make brave learners.
