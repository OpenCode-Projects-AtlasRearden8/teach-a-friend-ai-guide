# Chapter 5 — Run the Session

The one-hour shape. It's not a script to recite — it's a skeleton you fill
with your friend's voice. If a section runs long, shorten another; if the
click comes early, you may finish early. The bones:

```
00–05  Arrive and lower the bar
05–10  Show, don't tell (one live demo, small and honest)
10–40  The first real task (they type)
40–50  Everybody explores: side quests, questions, the "what if" game
50–60  Write down the one thing, celebrate, book the next touch, stop
```

## 5.1 Minute 0–5: Arrive and lower the bar

Open with the low-expectations sentence (Chapter 1, §1.4), adapted:

> "Alright — one hour, one real thing, and if you walk out thinking it's
> not for you, that's a perfectly good ending. Ready? No quiz, no homework.
> You get coffee; I brought cookies."

Then get them in the seat. **The seat matters: their machine (or yours, for
the very first ten minutes, at *their* pace), them on the keyboard, you
beside — not behind.** Beside says *colleague*; behind says *proctor*.

## 5.2 Minute 5–10: Show, don't tell

One small live demo. Not a tour — a *single trick*, chosen live:

- Bad demo: walking through menus, features, model names, pricing tiers.
- Good demo: "Here's what I did this morning —" and you ask it one question
  you actually had today, right in front of them. Real, small, useful.

After the demo, immediately: **"Okay — your turn. Same thing, but about
whatever's in your week. You type."** The demo is over in three minutes of
your hands, then theirs.

## 5.3 Minute 10–40: The first real task

This is the golden half hour. How it usually unfolds:

**He or she freezes at the blank box.** Normal! Everyone does. Fill the
silence with one of the three sparks you brought (Chapter 2 §2.4): "want to
start with one of these, or type your own in your own words?" Typing in
their own words, messy grammar and all, is the strongest possible opening —
say so: "Typos are completely fine. It doesn't judge."

**They write a vague prompt and get a blah answer.** Also normal, and
valuable: now *editing a prompt* has meaning. Teach the upgrade by example:

> "What would you change about this answer?…… Right. Tell *it* that, same
> words."
Nothing teaches prompting faster than copy-editing an answer they care
about.

**The answer is too long, too formal, too cheerful.** Perfect teaching
material. "Tell it: shorter, friendlier, and say it like a normal person." They
learn that follow-up instructions are normal — this is a conversation, not a
vending machine.

**You want to grab the keyboard.** Don't. If something needs demoing,
demonstrate on *your own device* or narrate *their* clicks: "What comes
next? Yeah. Type it."

**Mid-task wobbles.** See §5.5 for the wobble menu.

## 5.4 The rules of the first session

1. **Their hands.** Yours are allowed on the trackpad for exactly one
   pointing gesture every ten minutes.
2. **Their words.** If they would say it only one way, honor that way. Never
   upgrade their prompt to "what a power user would write" while watching.
3. **Applaud the process, not the product.** "You just asked it to change
   its mind — that's a power move," not "wow, that poem is amazing."
4. **Two-minutes-and-stuck rule.** If they've been stuck two minutes, offer
   a joint attempt: "want to take a run at it together?" — not the answer.
5. **The ‘dumb question’ at minute 20/30/45.** If they say "this is probably
   a dumb question," the answer you say is always: "Nope — smart people ask
   that all the time, and here's why it's a great question: …" and give a
   real reason it's smart. Never rush this moment.

## 5.5 The wobble menu

When something goes sideways, choose from this list, in order:

- **"Huh."** (Out loud, without alarm.) A canvas reset: you saying this is
  normal, we're curious, next move is a click away.
- **"That's my fault —"** if you instructed it. Then fix it together.
  Honest bug ownership builds trust faster than flawless.
- **The experiment move.** "What happens if we just ask it
  again in a different way?" Retry as an *inquiring experiment*, not a chore.
- **"We park that one."** Some things just fail inside an hour. Say it like
  a parking lot: "Not today's enemy. On we go." Failure parked ≠ failure
  owned.
- **"Let's look at what it actually said."** When the mistake is the AI's,
  read the nonsense *together, amused.* Finding where exactly it went wrong
  is one of the best lessons in the whole manual — and it usually deserves
  an actual laugh.

## 5.6 Minute 40–50: Everybody explores

Toward the end of the first task, open the floor:

- **The "what if" game.** "What would you ask it if there were no wrong
  questions?" Their pipedreams are where session two lives.
- **One side quest,** max, from their life — the yearbook photo question,
  the chef question, whatever. Keep it five minutes. **They type this one
  100% solo** (you literally fold your hands). Their first *independent*
  exchange should happen while you're still in the building.
- **The fear question,** if it hasn't arisen: "Anything about this bug you?
  Privacy keeping you up?" Answer honestly in plain terms, including what
  you *don't* know.

## 5.7 Minute 50–60: Written by their hand

End every session the same way:

1. **We write down one thing.** On paper, in *their* writing: the one move
   from today (e.g., "type it like a text, then tell it to make it prettier").
   One thing, not five. Paper over screenshots — it's offline, theirs,
   tangibly theirs.
2. **Celebrate the win** (how to do this well is the entire Chapter 6 — get
   it right; it's the part people remember all week).
3. **Set the simple between-move:** "One question to it before Friday.
   Anything. Tell me what you asked when we talk." A friendly experiment,
   not homework.
4. **Name the next touch.** Vague: "let's do more soon" dies. Concrete:
   "same time next Saturday?" survives.
5. **Stop. On time. Even mid-fun.** Wounds to the fun—" *just a bit
   longer,*" " *ooh let me show you this one more thing,*" — are how the
   whole hour becomes a blur they vaguely enjoyed and can't repeat. End
   while they want more.

## 5.8 After they leave

- Text them one line, warm and short: "You drove that whole card-writing
  thing yourself. Sister's gonna love it." (Chapter 6 has the full art.)
- Jot three lines in your own session log: what clicked, what stuck, what
  to try next time. Thirty seconds now saves an hour before session two.

## Chapter 5 in one breath

Arrive low, demo small, hand them the keys in minute ten, wobble with
grace, explore their imagination near the end, end with one written thing
and a warm text — and stop the clock on time, every time.
