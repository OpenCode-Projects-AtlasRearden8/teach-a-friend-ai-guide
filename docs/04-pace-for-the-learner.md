# Chapter 4 — Pace for the Learner

Different learners need different gears. This chapter maps the five learner
types you'll most likely help, with what to do differently for each. Read the
section for your person before every session; the rest is there when they
surprise you.

## 4.0 The universal throttle

Whatever the type, these hold true:

- **They type.** Always, from minute one, no matter the learner.
- **One thing at a time.** No menus unless they ask.
- **Wait through the silence.** After you ask anything, count slowly to five
  before speaking again. Most of learning happens in that gap.
- **Stop at 10/10, not 8/10.** End each hour on a clean win, even a small one.

## 4.1 The senior

**Who they are:** Often sharp and experienced — they ran households, offices,
factories, or classrooms. What's new isn't intelligence; it's vocabulary and
ease. Fear of "breaking something" is usually the loudest thing in the room.

**Pace:** Slow and steady. Double your pauses. One concept per hour, woven
into the task rather than explained apart from it.

**Do:**

- Use paper. Writing down the three steps of today's task, in their
  handwriting, on a real notepad, is dignity and memory aid in one.
- Bigger text, standing distance from the screen, chair with arms.
- Name things plainly: "the chat box," "the send arrow." Screen words should
  describe location ("the box at the bottom"), not brand UI.
- Say the *why* of each small step: "We're hitting refresh because sometimes
  it just gets stuck. Like the printer, no judgment."
- Welcome the tangents. The story they tell while looking at the screen is
  not a delay — it's often the bridge to the perfect prompt.
- Revisit, don't advance, if last week rusted. A fifth of a step forward with
  confidence beats a whole step with wobbling.

**Avoid:**

- Speed-typing over their shoulder narration ("click, now scroll, now
  type…"). Says: *the steps are so trivial I'll keep a running commentary.*
- Pretending keyboard shortcuts are obligatory. Ctrl+V is a bonus, never a
  gate.
- "It's easy" — even said kindly, it quietly reframes any struggle as their
  failure.
- Removing their struggle entirely. When you feel the pull to take the
  keyboard, offer: "Want to take a run at it, or should we do it together?"

**A senior's favorite sentence:** "Let's do it together, slowly."

## 4.2 The skeptical spouse or partner

**Who they are:** See through hype by reflex. May have said, verbatim,
"This AI thing is a bubble," or "I don't need a computer to think for me."
Their skepticism is *intelligence*, not an obstacle. It's also armor over
quiet worry: *everyone else seems fast-forwarded and I'm standing still.*

**Pace:** Curious, specific, zero evangelism. Let the tool win or lose on
facts. Keep your tone light — you're a scout reporting back, not a salesperson.

**Do:**

- Lead with their domain, their skepticism applied *for* them: "Everyone
  says it thinks. It doesn't. It's a pattern finisher — brilliant at drafts,
  confidently wrong at facts. Let's test that on your stuff."
- **Let them try to break it.** Skeptics relax once they've caught the AI
  in an error and lived. Hand it over: "Try to make it say something
  wrong. Genuinely. I'll wait."
- Use their language. If they say "it's autocorrect on steroids," *use the
  autocorrect metaphor* and show where the analogy holds and snaps.
- Keep the first task small and *testable*: drafts, lists, summaries —
  things they can verify in ten seconds against their own judgment.
- When it turns out to be useful, resist "I told you so." Say instead:
  "Yeah — turns out it's decent at *this one thing.* That's all it needed
  to be."

**Avoid:**

- Wonder and hype. "It's amazing," "the future," "you won't believe it."
- Turning their hour into a debate. One or two skeptic-parries, then back to
  the task.
- Surveys of the AI ecosystem, model names, or what's coming next year.

**A skeptic's favorite sentence:** "It's just a draft-finisher — let's
grade its homework."

## 4.3 The student (teen or young adult)

**Who they are:** Digitally fluent and socially anxious about using it
"right." Their fear isn't clicking; it's *identity*: getting caught using AI
for homework ("is that cheating?"), or feeling like a fraud.

**Pace:** Fast on mechanics, slow on judgment. The tech needs seconds; the
ethics and self-respect parts take real minutes.

**Do:**

- Talk honestly about honesty: "The teacher-question isn't 'did AI touch
  this,' it's 'does this represent you?' Let's learn to use it as a tutor —
  explainer, practice-quizzer, feedback-giver — and keep your name on
  what's yours."
- Show the tutor loop first: "explain this like I'm confused, quiz me,
  tell me what I got wrong and why." That loop is a *lifetime grade
  raiser* and 100% defensible.
- Teach the "draft the way *you* would, ask AI to crit it like a tough
  teacher" move. Feedback on your work ≠ your work.
- Give them language for the family and school conversations: how to
  explain what they did, how to say no to the shortcut.
- Keep the pace quick — they'll type fast and iterate. Your job is to slow
  *their* thinking down, not their fingers.

**Avoid:**

- Moralizing in either direction. Neither "AI is corrupting your mind" nor
  "AI will 10x your GPA."
- Adopting their slang with full commitment. You are not their peer. Warm,
  slightly amused, occasionally forty years out of date — that's the vibe.
- Long speeches. They'll zone out past 45 seconds. No shame — you would too.

**A student's favorite sentence:** "Let's have it tutor you, not do it for
you — that's the trick nobody teaches."

## 4.4 The busy friend

**Who they are:** Wants the value, refuses the journey. "Just show me the
one thing." Often the easiest session and the highest dropout rate if
nothing sticks into their week.

**Pace:** ruthless efficiency. The whole first hour is one task, done to
done. Practice is *embedded in their week*, not added to it.

**Do:**

- Compress: 40 focused minutes, not 60+.
- Anchor every practice to an existing habit: "a phone-habit AI question
  every Monday morning while coffee brews," "one per checkout line."
- Give them one saved prompt they'll reuse forever (Appendix 8).
- Debrief on voice memo between meetings, if needed.

**Avoid:**

- "Foundation" anything: no background, no explanation of how models work.
- Assigning practice. Offer optional rituals instead; busy people already
  live with a to-do list longer than any human's attention.

**A busy friend's favorite sentence:** "Forty minutes, one thing, done
properly."

## 4.5 The self-taught dabbler

**Who they are:** Uses AI already, but with holes. Confident on some things,
mystery-on-others (saving, privacy, undo, "where did my chats go," paying vs
not paying). Their copy-paste chain-of-prompts workflow is quietly fragile.

**Pace:** Respectful upgrade. You're not teaching from zero — you're sealing
leaks.

**Do:**

- Ask what they already do; genuinely listen; learn something.
- Patch the *specific* leaks: chat history, folders/projects, editing vs.
  regenerating, share-link privacy, "why do answers differ on repeat."
- Introduce one power move at a time, tied to their existing flow.
- Say "I'd not seen that" whenever it's true — respect travels in circles:
  if you treat them as competent, they can afford to ask beginner questions.

**Avoid:**

- Re-teaching basics they've mentally outgrown. They'll resent it.
- Explaining their own workflow to them ("well actually the way you do it…").
  One-sentence observations beat paragraph corrections.

**A dabbler's favorite sentence:** "What's your current flow? Let's see
where the friction hides."

## 4.6 When you're not sure which type you have

Most people are a blend (the skeptic-dabbler is common; the senior-student is
delightful). In that case:

1. First ten minutes: watch, don't steer. Type/Mouse confidence tells you the
   mechanics layer. Question style tells you the trust layer.
2. Ask one diagnostic question: "What do you *hope* this thing can do for
   you — and what do you suspect it'll be annoying about?"
3. Blend the pace accordingly: mechanics at the faster gear, judgment at the
   slower one.

## Chapter 4 in one breath

Read the person, set the gear — slow for seniors, curious for skeptics, fast
mechanics with slow judgment for students, ruthless for the busy, respectful
upgrades for dabblers. The universal throttle underneath: they type, one
thing at a time, five-second silences, clean wins.
