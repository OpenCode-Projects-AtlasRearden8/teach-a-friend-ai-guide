# Chapter 1 — The Heart of Helping

Before any technique, there's a stance. This chapter is about who you are in
the room, because the person across from you will read that before they read
anything on the screen.

## 1.1 Patience over pride

The biggest risk in teaching a friend isn't boredom or confusion — it's
*your own competence*. You know how to do this. The moment you start showing
off how much you know, the session quietly turns into a demo, and the person
stops feeling like the main character.

Signs your pride is creeping in:

- You start sentences with "All you have to do is…"
- You answer questions they haven't asked yet, three steps ahead.
- You reach for the mouse "just to show you real quick" — for the third time.
- Their questions get shorter, and they nod more.

None of these make you a bad person. They just mean the hour is drifting from
*their* learning to *your* performance. Notice it, gently, and hand the
keyboard back.

## 1.2 Curiosity over correction

When your friend does something "wrong" — types the whole thing in the search
bar, asks the AI the question in a weird way, says "hey" to it like a person —
curiosity beats correction almost every time.

| Reaction | Sounds like | How it lands |
|---|---|---|
| Correction | "No no, you don't need to say please." | "I did it wrong." |
| Curiosity | "Ha, actually that works fine — the AI doesn't mind. Let's see what it does." | "My instincts are okay here." |

Beginner instincts are often *better* than experienced-user instincts for the
first hour. Saying hello to the AI before asking a question makes responses
warmer and clearer. Saying "please" costs nothing. Resist the urge to optimize
their style; the goal of session one is not the perfect prompt. It's the
feeling of **"oh — I get to just talk to this thing."**

## 1.3 Never in a position to feel dumb

The only rule with no exceptions. Pay attention to the little moves that keep
the room safe:

- **Say "we."** "Let's take a look" instead of "you need to look."
- **Fail in the open, on purpose.** When something you try doesn't work, say
  "huh, that didn't work — see, it's normal, we just try another way." Your
  visible, unbothered failure is permission for theirs.
- **Match their pace, not yours.** If they're reading slowly, they're
  *reading*. That's learning, not slowness.
- **Normalize the confusion.** "Everyone finds that weird at first. There are
  people with master's degrees who find this weird."
- **Never compare.** Not to yourself, not to "my dad figured this out in five
  minutes," and *especially* not to other people their age.

## 1.4 The kindness of low expectations

Counterintuitive truth: people relax when you set expectations *low and
concrete*, not when you promise miracles.

Say this: **"By the end of the hour, you'll have done one real thing — we'll
see what it's like, and you don't have to like it. If you hate it, you never
have to touch it again."**

That sentence does four things:

1. It makes the goal small and finishable.
2. It gives them a genuine, no-pressure exit.
3. It signals this is *their* decision.
4. It quietly removes your need to sell them on anything.

## 1.5 The helper's dignity (yes, yours too)

You are allowed to not know things. You are allowed to say "I don't know,
let's find out together" — that sentence is actually one of the most
confidence-building things in the whole hour, because it demonstrates *how
experts handle not knowing.*

You're also allowed to have a bad day. If you're exhausted, anxious, or
impatient with the world today, reschedule. One warm hour beats two tired
ones. Never teach a hangry session.

## 1.6 A short practice for before every session

Ninety seconds, alone, before they arrive:

1. **Remember their wins** from last time. Name two.
2. **Pick one task.** Not one topic — one *task* (Chapter 3).
3. **Set the low bar.** Decide the sentence you'll open with (Chapter 5).
4. **Breathe.** You're not here to impress. You're here to open a door.

## Chapter 1 in one breath

Be patient with the pace, curious about their instincts, careful about their
dignity, and honest about the smallness — and the greatness — of the first
hour. You're not teaching a syllabus. You're teaching one person that this
strange new world has a door marked *for you, too.*
