# Chapter 7 — Hand Off Safely

The helping run isn't done when your friend is competent. It's done when they
*own the journey* — and when you've made your exit without leaving a mess
behind. This chapter is the graceful ending: when to hand off, how to do it,
and how to make sure the whole thing stays safe and private.

## 7.1 Why you must hand off

Staying past the peak helps nobody:

- **It steals their competence.** Every question you answer forever is a
  question they stop learning to answer. The helper's unfinished job
  becomes their dependency.
- **It exhausts you.** Helpers burn out. Burned-out helpers get snappy. One
  sharp sentence and two years of trust evaporates.
- **It distorts the friendship.** Every hangout starts to orbit a
  laptop. Nobody wants to be *the project* of a friendship.

The end state is beautiful and modest: **they use AI on their own, mostly
without you — and when they ask you something now and then, it's out of
curiosity, not need.**

## 7.2 The signs it's time (checklist)

Five or more of these mean the handoff conversation is due:

- [ ] They solve the two-minute stumble alone — and keep going.
- [ ] They bring *their own* tasks to sessions now.
- [ ] They've corrected you at least once. (Gold. Worth pausing to enjoy.)
- [ ] Sessions feel a bit like small talk with a laptop open.
- [ ] They've used AI on something you know nothing about.
- [ ] They've made a thing they're proud of and *shown someone else*.
- [ ] They've settled privacy/what-not-to-share rules without prompting.
- [ ] They've helped *someone else* in their life do one thing.

## 7.3 The handoff conversation

Ten minutes, at the end of a session (the handoff session, Chapter 6 §6.6).
Sitting across the table, no laptop open. Speak plainly:

> "You've got this now. The thing I've loved watching these last weeks is
> that you don't need me for it anymore — that was the whole point. From
> here, this thing is yours. If you ever hit something weird, ask me, but
> you're the proud owner of this, not a student in it."

Then make the ending *real* by marking it:

- **The ownership moment:** hand over their wins journal, current one into
  their hands, not a "someday rebind." Tell them the next page is blank
  on purpose.
- **The permission slip:** name what they're now allowed to do *without
  telling you*: pick tasks, quit tools, ignore AI for a month, use it for
  jobs you'd never choose.
- **The failure-proofing promise:** "If something goes wrong — really
  wrong, a bill, a purchase, anything scary — call me even if you think
  you caused it. Honest mistakes are almost always fixable."
- **The revised role:** spell out the new you. "I'm not your AI guy.
  I'm the guy you can show cool stuff to whenever you want. I'll mostly
  say 'that's brilliant, how'd you do it?' and mean it."

A handoff without a *moment* is a rumor; without a *promise*, it leaves
shame-flavored silence for the next stumble. Do both.

## 7.4 Permission slips for ending states

"Handoff" doesn't always mean full graduation. Learners can also land in
these states, and no shame attaches to any of them:

- **The hobbyist:** uses AI for fun only. Fine. Their wins journal can
  contain forty recipes.
- **The occasional-er:** opens it twice a year for a specific job type
  (taxes-adjacent letters). Fine. Write their recipe on the fridge.
- **The refuser:** tried it honestly, decided it's not for them. *Fine.*
  Say so out loud: "You gave it a real shot, and now you're the most
  informed AI-skeptic I know. That's an excellent outcome." Low-pressure
  exits keep curiosity alive; full-court presses bury it.
- **The joiner-of-classes:** they now want a *structured class* (that's a
  different product than a friend!). Point them to your local library,
  senior center, or community college course, and wave goodbye happily.

Check your helping run didn't quietly force a single destination. Multiple happy
landings exist.

## 7.5 Safety and privacy hygiene (both of you)

Before you exit, these should be true — check them explicitly in the final
sessions:

**Their side:**

- They know **what not to share**: full street addresses, ID/passport/
  insurance numbers, medical record numbers, banking details, passwords
  (obviously or cleverly disguised), other people's private info.
- They know **it's a stranger's computer.** What gets typed is sent to a
  company's servers; specifics vary by tool, so the simple rule is: *past
  a certain intimacy threshold, don't type it at all.*
- They know **answers can be wrong**, confidently, and that the right
  response is verify-for-stakes: fun stuff, trust the flow; money/health/
  law, trust a professional or an official source.
- They know **where their stuff lives**: chat history, saved files, what
  happens if they delete, what share-links reveal to recipients.
- They have **the cost picture**: what's free, what's paid, what happens
  to a free account's limits, and how to say no to an upgrade nudge.
- Their account is **theirs**: their email, their password, no share-and-
  tell. (This is why Chapter 2 §2.6 matters — you built it this way on
  purpose.)

**Your side:**

- **You don't hold their credentials.** Ever. Not "temporarily," not in a
  note, not in a shared last pass.
- **You don't routinely look at their chats.** If they show you something,
  fine; routine audit is spying wearing a teacher costume.
- **You keep no copies** of their documents/prompts unless they asked.
- **You're off the hook for outcomes.** You're a friend who taught a tool —
  you never signed up for being a warranty. Say this kindly if they lean
  in: "I'll always be here *as a friend*; this tool's output is yours to
  judge — you're better at judging your life than it is."
- **No money.** If they offer to pay, decline warmly: "My fee is you
  showing me your grandkid's AI-printed birthday card." Gift reciprocation:
  coffee, cookies, card — lovely. Cash: no, ever.

## 7.6 After the handoff

- **Stay in curiosity mode.** Ask "have you used the thing lately?" out of
  *interest*, roughly twice a year, never as an audit.
- **Accept the new shape of questions:** risk questions migrate to
  public-ish spaces ("can you check this email?"), curiosity questions stay
  private. Honor both.
- **Let some drift happen.** If they stop using it entirely for a season,
  that's not failure, it's weather.
- **Take the win yourself.** You opened a door and *walked away from the
  doorway afterwards.* A large, generous act with a small footprint. Notice
  it. You've done the thing exactly right.

## 7.7 When THEY hand off to YOU

The mirror case, worth thirty lines: sometimes *you're* the learner and
*they're* the helper — the chess-nephew teaching you the engine, the
teenager teaching you a video tool. Then all the advice inverts, and the
rhythm is: express *honestly-held* ignorance, type everything yourself,
ask the patronizing questions before anyone else can, and celebrate *their*
teaching with move-credit ("you just explained that better than the
tutorial"). The manual ends where it began: never make the person feel
dumb — in either chair.

## Chapter 7 in one breath

Watch the checklist, hold the ten-minute conversation, hand over the journal
with the blank page, promise rescue for the scary mistakes, keep the privacy
hygiene bidirectional — and exit while the door still swings both ways.
