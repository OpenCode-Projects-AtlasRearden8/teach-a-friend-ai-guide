Chapter 3 — Pick the First Real Task

The first task is the single highest-leverage decision of the whole helping run. Pick the right one and the "click" happens inside twenty minutes. Pick the wrong one and AI becomes "that thing that writes essays weirdly."

3.1 What makes a first task "real"

A Real First Task has all six of these:

  1. It's theirs. A project they actually care about, not a demo you like.
  2. It completes inside the hour. A visible finish line, even a small one.
  3. It touches their real life. Something they'd do anyway — a card, a plan, a list, a letter.
  4. It shows, not tells. When it works, the value is felt, not explained.
  5. It has a restart friendly failure mode. If it goes sideways, there's an obvious, low-drama way to try again.
  6. They type at least half of it. Their hands on the keys in the first ten minutes.

3.2 The task picker

Ask your friend three questions, out loud, casually:

  1. "What did you spend time on this week that was a bit of a slog?"
  2. "What do you keep meaning to do, but never get around to?"
  3. "If a very fast, tireless helper worked at your kitchen table, what would you hand it first?"

Their answers map to tasks. Match the shape of the chore:

They said… Task shape Example first task
"Writing cards/messages honestly takes me forever." Drafting "Write a birthday card to my sister that sounds like me, not like a card."
"I have no idea what to cook anymore." Planning "Give us five dinner ideas from what's in the fridge, with a shopping list."
"I'm terrified of email to the insurance/city/bank." Assertive drafting "Help me write a firm, polite email asking about this bill."
"I never remember what the doctor said / what the plan was." Summarizing & organizing "Turn my messy notes into a one-page plan I can actually follow."
"Small talk with new people is hard." Practice "Help me think of five things to say at my nephew's graduation party."
"I want to sell my stuff but listings take hours." Transforming "Write a clear, honest eBay listing for this lamp from these rough notes."
"I've been meaning to learn chess/my family tree/budgeting for years." Learning partner "Teach me the rules of chess with mini-games, 15 minutes a day."

If the answers are all "nothing really," borrow one of the examples — but still anchor it to a real upcoming event in their life (a birthday, a trip, a repair). Real dates make real stakes.

3.3 Veteran tasks — the ones that click for almost everyone

When in doubt, these are reliable openers, ordered by click-rate in the wild:

  1. The card or message from the heart. They describe the person in clumsy sentences; AI helps shape it; tears (good tears) optional; conversion almost guaranteed. Pro move: have them dictate to you first, then type the prompt together.
  2. The kitchen rescue. "Here's what's in my fridge. Give me five dinners for two picky eaters, 30 minutes max." Instant, tangible, delicious.
  3. The dreaded email. Everyone has one. Turning a mountain of dread into a five-line email they can edit in two minutes is viscerally powerful.
  4. The trip sketch. Not a whole itinerary — a first-take day plan for a real upcoming trip.
  5. The "explain this letter" moment. A confusing bill, lease clause, or form. AI reads it with them, at their speed, and they come out feeling sharper, not dumber.

3.4 Tasks to avoid on purpose

Some "obvious" first tasks are traps. Avoid for session one:

  • Anything demo-y. "Ask it to write a poem about your cat." Cute, but untethered from their life; feels like a toy, not a tool. (Poems are great as an accent, not as the task.)
  • Anything with stakes they can't afford. Medical decisions, legal documents, "should I sell my house." The accuracy anxiety eats the magic, and it drifts toward fourth-opinion territory.
  • Anything requiring accounts you didn't set up (Chapter 2 §2.6).
  • Anything multi-tool. One AI, one task. Save the tool tour.
  • Your favorite workflow. It's yours. They won't love it because you do.

3.5 The click

You'll know it happened. It's not the smile. It's the pause — the moment they stop narrating to you and start talking to the AI, mid-blink, as if you've left the room. Sometimes they even lean in. The pause after the first real answer is the most sacred moment of a helping run. Don't narrate over it. Let it sit. Then say something like: "That's the thing people keep trying to explain. It's just… that."

3.6 The backup task

Have one. If the first task resolves astonishingly fast (great!) or hits a wall (fine), pivot without ceremony: "Okay, next trick — this one's actually more fun." Never let the hour end on "well… that didn't work." Work always ends on a window, never a wall.

Chapter 3 in one breath

One task, theirs, finished inside the hour, felt-not-explained, half of it typed by them — backed up by a second one. Choose the chore whose shape fits their week; the tool will win their heart on its own.
